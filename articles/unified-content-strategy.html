What We Learned Building a Unified Content Program

Several teams were creating material for the same product. A small pilot helped us see the overlap and divide the work more clearly.

By Sharmila Das
2026
8 minute read

At first, the problem looked like too much content. Marketing, technical publications, customer education, support, sales enablement, and product teams were all creating material for the same product and the same customer. Each group had its own brief, tools, deadlines, and measures. The individual pieces were finished, but customers still encountered repeated explanations, conflicting guidance, and gaps.

We needed a practical way for the teams to coordinate. They had to agree on the customer need, the source information, ownership, review, reuse, and measures. Each group could still choose the format and tool that fit its audience. The decisions that affected everyone had to be made together.

Look past the list of deliverables

Content plans often begin as lists: user guides, web pages, training modules, videos, sales presentations, application notes, and support articles. The list shows what must be produced, but it hides how much of the underlying work is the same.

One customer question may appear in a manual, tutorial, support article, field-training module, and interactive guide. When each team starts from scratch, the company pays several times for research, writing, review, localization, maintenance, and correction.

We started by identifying the explanation, claim, procedure, or concept behind each format. Then we asked who should create it, who should review it, who would maintain it, and how other teams could adapt it. Ownership became easier to discuss because the conversation focused on the information itself.

The seven steps we used

1. Understand the business

We began with product priorities, cost pressures, regulations, and the customer problems that mattered most. This kept the content work tied to a real need.

2. Inventory enough content to see the patterns

We captured location, owner, audience, format, channel, lifecycle state, and performance when it was available. We stopped when the inventory could show duplicates, contradictions, gaps, old material, and useful candidates for reuse.

3. Study the audience and journey

We focused on the audience’s role, task, questions, barriers, and preferred access point. We then connected those needs to discovery, evaluation, onboarding, use, troubleshooting, and adoption.

4. Check quality and reuse

We reviewed accuracy, currency, clarity, findability, accessibility, and actual use. We also checked who owned the information, whether the format limited reuse, and when the material should be reviewed again.

5. Measure customer use and team effort

Customer measures included search behavior, completion, support calls, task success, and feedback. Team measures included cycle time, review effort, rework, duplicate production, and reuse.

6. Plan the work

Each topic was connected to its audience need, source, journey stage, channel, owner, and timing. We prioritized by customer value, risk, reuse, dependencies, and effort.

7. Publish and follow up

At launch, we confirmed ownership, links, metadata, analytics, source, and version. After launch, we reviewed performance, improved weak material, retired old content, and carried the findings into the next release.

What the audit uncovered

We reviewed product and engineering source material alongside finished content and planned deliverables. A manual comparison found roughly 30 percent overlap in the material we examined.

The 30 percent overlap gave the teams a concrete reason to change how they worked.

Teams could see where they were repeating research, where material could be reused, and where missing content appeared too late. The audit also changed the ownership discussion. Instead of asking which team owned a document, we asked who should be responsible for the underlying information and which teams needed to adapt it for their audiences.

How the pilot worked

We began with a group kickoff, a common set of requirements, and weekly coordination. Teams identified the best available inputs, assigned work to the people with the right expertise, coordinated reviews, and recorded what they learned.

One approved topic could support a user manual, tutorial, pre-sales training, eLearning module, field training, or interactive manual. Each team still shaped the final experience for its audience.

We did not require every contributor to use the same structured-authoring tool. Methods such as DITA still mattered, but occasional contributors needed an easier entry point. Teams used familiar collaboration tools where needed, supported by templates, content models, ownership rules, and editorial help.

What changed

  • Teams could see overlap, gaps, dependencies, and ownership more clearly.
  • Work moved to the groups best equipped to create and maintain the information.
  • The same audit and planning method could be used for later product launches.
  • The pilot findings supported the case for better content collaboration and CMS capabilities.

By the end of the pilot, teams had a repeatable way to identify needs, compare work, assign ownership, plan reuse, and measure the results.

Four lessons for leaders

Ownership affects adoption

Changes to tools, budgets, recognition, and control affect how people respond. Name the decision owners early and explain how the process uses each team’s expertise.

Make the process easy to enter

Occasional contributors need clear templates, guided steps, and editorial support. Strong structure should not require every person to become an expert in the authoring tool.

Sponsors need to settle roadblocks

Teams can resolve many issues together. Ownership and platform choices sometimes require a leader to decide. Sponsors should confirm priorities and step in when a disagreement stalls the work.

Use the same measures

Customer satisfaction, task success, cycle time, review effort, reuse, and support impact give teams a practical basis for choosing among formats and workflows.

Where to begin

Choose one customer journey and bring together the teams that create content for it. Compare the material, identify repeated work, agree on ownership, and test a simpler process. Track review time, reuse, customer success, and support impact. Those results will show where the approach is worth expanding.
