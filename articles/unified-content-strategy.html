What We Learned Building a Unified Content Program

Several teams were creating material for the same product. A small pilot helped us see the overlap and divide the work more clearly.

By Sharmila Das
2026
8 minute read

The launch plan looked complete. Every team had deliverables, dates, and owners. Yet the same explanation was being researched and reviewed in several places, while a few customer questions had no clear home at all.

Marketing, technical publications, customer education, support, sales enablement, and product were each working from a different brief. None of those briefs was unreasonable. Taken together, they produced duplicate work, uneven guidance, and a maintenance problem waiting for the next release.

Look past the list of deliverables

A deliverables list can conceal the real unit of work. “User guide,” “training module,” and “support article” sound like three separate assignments. Often they depend on the same product explanation, procedure, or approved claim.

That distinction changed the conversation. Instead of arguing over who owned a document, we could ask who was closest to the underlying information, who could approve it, and which teams needed to reshape it for a particular audience. The format still mattered, just later.

The working method

1. Understand the business

We began with product priorities, cost pressures, regulatory constraints, and the customer problems people were already trying to solve. That gave us a reason to stop debating low-value material.

2. Inventory enough content to see the patterns

We recorded location, owner, audience, format, channel, lifecycle state, and whatever performance data existed. The inventory was deliberately bounded. Once it showed duplication, contradictions, gaps, stale material, and plausible reuse, it had done its job.

3. Study the audience and journey

Job titles were not enough. We looked at the task, the question behind it, what got in the way, and where someone would naturally look for help. Those needs were mapped across discovery, evaluation, onboarding, everyday use, and troubleshooting.

4. Check quality and reuse

Accuracy and currency came first, followed by clarity, findability, accessibility, and evidence of use. We also noted when a page-based format made a useful explanation hard to reuse.

5. Measure customer use and team effort

Customer signals included search behavior, completion, support contacts, task success, and direct feedback. For the teams, we watched cycle time, review effort, rework, duplicate production, and reuse. Not every signal was available at the start.

6. Plan the work

Each topic was connected to an audience need, a source, a journey stage, a channel, an owner, and a date. Priority came from customer value and risk first, then reuse potential, dependencies, and effort.

7. Publish and follow up

Before launch we checked ownership, links, metadata, analytics, source, and version. Afterward came the less glamorous work: fixing weak material, retiring old pages, and carrying unresolved questions into the next release.

What the audit uncovered

We placed product and engineering sources beside finished content and work already planned for the launch. The manual comparison found roughly 30 percent overlap in the sample.

Thirty percent was large enough to make duplication visible—and small enough to address without redesigning every workflow at once.

The useful part was not the percentage by itself. Teams could point to repeated research and review, spot missing material earlier, and see where one approved explanation could support several outputs. Ownership became easier to discuss once it was attached to information instead of departmental territory.

How the pilot worked

The pilot used a shared kickoff, a common set of requirements, and a weekly working session. The meeting was not a status round-robin. People brought source conflicts, ownership questions, and upcoming reviews that could block another team.

One approved topic might feed a manual, a tutorial, pre-sales training, an eLearning module, and field enablement. The teams still wrote for their own audiences; reuse did not mean publishing the same paragraph everywhere.

We also resisted forcing every occasional contributor into the same structured-authoring tool. DITA and topic-based methods mattered for durable technical content. Other contributors needed a lighter entry point, with templates and editorial help doing more of the structural work.

What changed

  • Teams could see overlap, gaps, dependencies, and ownership more clearly.
  • Work moved to the groups best equipped to create and maintain the information.
  • The same audit and planning method could be used for later product launches.
  • The pilot findings supported the case for better content collaboration and CMS capabilities.

The process was still imperfect. Some ownership questions required sponsor decisions, and measurement was uneven. What the pilot produced was a repeatable way to surface those issues before the content reached customers.

What I would carry into the next program

Ownership affects adoption

Tools, budgets, recognition, and control are tied to identity. A reuse proposal can sound like a proposal to take work away. Name decision owners early and be direct about what each team still controls.

Make the process easy to enter

Occasional contributors need clear templates, a short path through the work, and access to editorial support. Good structure should not depend on everyone becoming an authoring-tool specialist.

Sponsors need to settle roadblocks

Working groups can settle many details. They usually cannot settle a disputed mandate or platform choice. Sponsors need to make those calls while the answer can still affect the plan.

Use the same measures

A shared set of measures gives teams something firmer than preference when they compare formats and workflows. The exact mix will vary, but customer success and team effort both belong in the decision.

Where to begin

Begin with one customer journey and the teams already producing content for it. Put the source material and planned deliverables on the same table. The first useful outcome may be a reuse opportunity; it may also be a contradiction that nobody had noticed. Either one is better found before launch.
