From Documentation Quality to a Knowledge Graph Prototype

Production quality controls and a separate graph prototype solve different parts of the knowledge problem.

By Sharmila Das
2026
3 minute read

In one production documentation program, I worked on content architecture and an automated quality system that checked more than 170 million words a month. Separately, at an internal hackathon, we built a knowledge graph proof of concept for developer documentation. Those were different levels of maturity, but they exposed the same issue: a content system needs to know what information is trustworthy and how pieces of it relate.

Structure the source material

The production content work defined models, taxonomy, metadata and templates. Those gave documentation consistent categories and made quality rules more repeatable. They also created a stronger base for finding relevant information than a pile of loosely organized pages.

Structure did not automatically capture every relationship. A concept page, API reference and troubleshooting procedure could be related even when their labels differed. That was the problem explored in the hackathon graph: making relationships explicit and testing whether retrieval could follow them rather than rely only on similar wording.

The proof of concept used reasoning loops and tool integrations to examine relationships across publicly available developer documentation. It was exploratory work, not a production graph serving the documentation program. I would not describe it as a deployed RAG pipeline or claim a particular vector store or embedding model without implementation evidence.

Connect generation to a content model

We also developed prompts around the content model and templates so that drafts had a known structure. That is a practical form of grounding: generation begins with a defined content type and relevant source context. It does not, by itself, prove that every generated claim was retrieved and cited through the graph.

The production quality system addressed a different point in the lifecycle. Its automated checks covered clarity, consistency, accessibility, search considerations and inclusivity across documentation. Those checks helped catch predictable issues before a person had to find them one by one. They complemented source structure and generation; they were not a substitute for verifying factual support.

Use feedback to find the upstream cause

Quality scores, suggestion acceptance and false positives can reveal whether a rule is useful. Repeated corrections also show where the content model, guidance or prompt needs work. In a future integrated system, I would trace a bad output from source through retrieval, generation and validation, then fix the earliest layer that caused it.

The useful lesson from these projects is the continuity of the discipline, not a claim that all components shipped as one architecture. Production governance proved the value of structured standards at scale. The graph prototype explored a deeper way to retrieve related knowledge. Together they informed how I would design the next system.
