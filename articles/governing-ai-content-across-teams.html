Governing AI Content Across Teams

A shared specification can align tools owned by different teams.

By Sharmila Das
2026
3 minute read

When several teams use AI to create customer content, each workflow can appear sound on its own. The trouble becomes visible when they make different claims about the same product or business. A content strategy lead may not own those tools, but can still define the standards they share.

Start with the content that reaches people

I would inventory the AI-assisted surfaces, their inputs, output types, owners and review points. I would sample actual outputs and trace disputed claims back to their sources. This shows where the problem is: a missing source, an outdated copy, weak retrieval, an unclear approval rule or a review step that comes too late.

The inventory should identify the few surfaces where inconsistency carries the most customer or brand risk. It should also show how a change to an approved claim reaches each tool. If the answer is “someone updates several prompts,” the system has a maintenance problem.

Define a shared specification

The common layer would describe approved knowledge objects, source authority, audience and market applicability, versions, expiration, provenance and review requirements. Platform teams can implement it in different ways. They still need a common meaning for “approved claim,” a way to reject expired material and a record of the evidence used in an output.

Creative teams should help define what belongs in the specification. Factual constraints and message priorities can be made explicit; judgment about originality, tone and cultural context still needs people. Governance works when it makes those decisions visible, not when it pretends they are identical.

Pilot one consequential workflow

I would choose one customer-facing surface, establish a baseline and test whether the shared specification improves it. Measures could include unsupported-claim rate, consistency across tools, the time needed to propagate a policy change, reviewer effort and trust from the business owners. A small pilot can also reveal whether the integration burden is reasonable for the teams that own the platforms.

The first 30 days would be diagnosis. The next 30 would turn findings into requirements with platform and creative partners. The final 30 would run a constrained pilot and make a case for what to change next. Those are proposed steps, not a claim that any particular organization has adopted this model.

I have worked on the underlying problem in content infrastructure: how to turn editorial standards into structured rules and checks that work at scale. The lesson I would carry forward is that governance needs a source, an owner and a route into each workflow. A policy document alone will not keep several AI systems aligned.
