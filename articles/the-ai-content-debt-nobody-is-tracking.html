The AI Content Debt Nobody Is Tracking

Copied guidance in separate AI workflows creates a maintenance problem.

By Sharmila Das
2026
3 minute read

AI experiments are easy to start. The copies of company knowledge they leave behind are harder to manage.

Generative AI has made it easy for teams to experiment, and I think that is a good thing.

A marketer can test a workflow without waiting for a platform roadmap. A regional team can solve a local problem. A creative group can try a new tool and learn quickly.

The part I would watch is what each experiment copies.

A team creates a prompt and pastes in product guidance. Another team adds brand rules. A third makes a regional version. Someone stores approved examples inside another tool. The product changes, but only some of those copies get updated.

Each decision made sense when it happened. Over time, the company ends up with several slightly different versions of its own guidance.

I think of that as AI content debt.

The first few workflows can hide the problem because knowledgeable people are close to them. They know which document is current. They notice a strange claim. They know when the output should not be trusted and fix it manually.

As more people use the system, that informal layer gets thinner.

Then basic questions become harder. Which customer definition should a tool use? Which claims are current? Where is the latest brand guidance? Can we trace a generated statement to its source? Who updates the system when a policy changes?

If every workflow answers those questions separately, every change has to be made separately too.

I would not respond by forcing every experiment onto one central platform. That can create its own problems and slow down useful work.

I would identify the information that should not be copied casually. Product facts are an obvious example. Policy is another. Some audience definitions, approved claims, source information and brand requirements may also need a shared home.

Teams can still build different workflows. They just should not have to recreate the company’s basic truth each time.

Fragmentation also makes learning harder.

Suppose one team discovers that a particular claim consistently confuses customers. It changes its prompt. Another team never sees the change and keeps using the claim. A third discovers the same issue later.

Several people have learned something. The company has not.

The correction might belong in the source guidance. It might belong in metadata, retrieval, an evaluation rule, or a local prompt. The important part is having a way to decide where the fix should live.

This is one reason I do not expect the model itself to be the most durable part of an enterprise AI system. Models can be changed. A company can buy access to a better one.

It takes much longer to build a reliable picture of your own products, customers, policies, decisions and quality standards.

Two companies can use the same model and get very different results because one has done that work and the other is still carrying copies of old guidance from experiment to experiment.

Fast experimentation is worth preserving. So is knowing where the company’s truth lives.
