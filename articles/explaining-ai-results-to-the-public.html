Explaining AI Results to the Public

How to describe a result and the limits of what it establishes

By Sharmila (Das) Rammohan
2026

Imagine a hypothetical announcement saying that an AI assistant completed a software task successfully. A reader cannot judge the result without knowing what the task involved. Did the assistant start from a prepared environment? Could it use external tools? How many attempts did it receive, and did a person intervene?

Those details belong in the explanation. They help readers understand what was demonstrated and whether it resembles the situations in which they might use the system.

Describe the test before predicting the impact

A benchmark score describes performance under particular conditions. Predictions about workplace or social impact require additional assumptions about deployment, access, tools, and human behavior. Writers should distinguish the measured result from those predictions.

For a tool-using assistant, describe what it could read or change. An assistant that drafts an email has different permissions from one that sends it. Calling both autonomous without explaining their permissions conceals a difference readers need to understand.

Uncertainty should be specific

Vague caveats such as “AI may make mistakes” are technically true and editorially weak. They do not tell a reader which claims are well established, which are extrapolations, or what kind of failure matters.

Name the uncertainty. Explain whether the result came from a controlled test or deployment, which conditions remain untested, and what would change the assessment. If the software assistant succeeded only with a prepared environment, say so near the success claim.

A specific limitation gives readers something they can apply when judging the result.

Explain how safeguards work

A statement that a system has oversight leaves several questions open. Who reviews its actions? Which actions require approval? Can a reviewer stop the process, and what happens after an error? Explain these procedures rather than relying on a general assurance.

In the email example, useful details would include whether the assistant can access attachments, whether recipients must be approved, and whether a person reviews the message before it is sent. These are illustrative controls; they should be described as implemented safeguards only when there is evidence that they are in place.

The reader should be able to test the claim

Link the result to its evidence. A research paper, evaluation report, or system card should let readers see the method and relevant limitations. Explain when a public summary leaves out information and how that limits independent assessment.

Model cards document intended uses, evaluation conditions, and limitations [1]. Public announcements can use the same approach: identify what was tested, what happened, and which uses the result does not establish.

Review major AI claims

Before publishing a substantial claim about advanced AI, an editorial desk should be able to answer six questions:

  1. What precisely is being claimed?
  2. What primary evidence supports it?
  3. Under what conditions was the evidence produced?
  4. What material limitation or disagreement should the reader know?
  5. Who reviewed the technical interpretation?
  6. What language would make the claim sound stronger than the evidence permits?

Check words such as understands, reasons, autonomous, safe, and human-level. If a test measured task completion, report task completion. A broader description needs a definition and evidence that supports it.

Give readers a way to check the evidence

Readers should be able to distinguish the observed result from the writer’s interpretation. State who conducted the test, identify the source, and explain any important limits on verification.

A clear announcement lets readers answer a few practical questions: what did the system do, under what conditions, and what remains untested? If those answers are missing, revise the claim before making it more persuasive.
