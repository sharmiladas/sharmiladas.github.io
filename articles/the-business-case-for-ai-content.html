AI content strategy

The Business Case for AI Content

Measure approved quality, time, reuse, campaign outcomes, and the full cost of the workflow.

By Sharmila Das
2026
3 minute read

An AI content proposal can promise faster production and still make the business worse off. If it creates more drafts than a team can review, repeats outdated claims, or adds a new platform cost without improving results, generation speed is a poor measure of value. I would make the investment case around approved, useful work and the full cost of producing it.

Establish the baseline

Before a pilot, I would measure the existing workflow: time to an approved campaign, first-pass acceptance, major rewrites, review hours, valid reuse, campaign outcomes, and total cost. I would also sample content against a rubric for strategic fit, audience relevance, message consistency, factual support, brand and channel fit. Those dimensions should be visible separately. An average score cannot excuse an unsupported claim.

This baseline makes the pilot answerable. Did the new workflow make approved work faster? Did quality hold or improve? Did people actually use it? Did the campaign perform at least as well against a comparable baseline or control?

Count value without double counting

The economic case can be expressed simply:

Net value = measurable savings and incremental contribution − full investment.

The savings and contribution may come from less rework, fewer review hours, valid reuse, an earlier launch that produces measurable contribution, or improved campaign performance. Each needs its own evidence and a clear boundary. For example, if the same saved review hour is counted under both quality and efficiency, the total is overstated. A quality score is a test result, not a dollar amount. I would translate better quality into money only when it measurably reduces work, errors, or risk, or changes an attributable business outcome.

The full investment includes model use, knowledge preparation, retrieval, evaluation, platform integration, governance, human review, maintenance, and the cost of running the pilot. The price of an API call is only a small part of the workflow cost.

A deliberately simple example

Suppose a pilot suggests that 500 campaigns a year would each require two fewer review hours. At a fully loaded cost of $100 per hour, that is $100,000 in annual review savings. Suppose 100 pieces of approved knowledge can each be reused at a net saving of $400 in separate creation work: another $40,000. If the full annual cost of the new workflow is $120,000, the illustrative net value is $20,000, or roughly 17% ROI. This calculation assumes those two savings pools do not overlap; the pilot would need to test that assumption.

The economic result is only eligible for a scale decision if quality gates pass, campaign performance does not deteriorate, and the workflow is adopted. If any of those fail, a positive spreadsheet is not enough.

Make the decision at the right scale

I would run a bounded pilot around one campaign workflow and define the scale criteria before it begins. Some benefits may be too uncertain to monetize credibly. An earlier launch, for example, has value only if it creates incremental contribution; days saved alone are not revenue. A reduction in error risk needs a defensible incident rate and cost, not an imagined large number.

Executives need a short decision: what problem we are solving, what changed in the pilot, what it cost, what remains uncertain, and whether the next investment is warranted. The aim is not to prove that AI is valuable in general. It is to show that this workflow produces more approved, effective content for its total cost.
