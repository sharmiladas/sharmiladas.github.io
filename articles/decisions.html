The Hard Part of AI Content Is Deciding What Ships

AI can produce a draft in seconds. Teams still have to decide whether the draft is true, useful, safe, and worth publishing.

By Sharmila Das
2026
6 minute read

A team had generated a healthy stack of campaign options, all fluent and on brief. Review stopped anyway. Two versions used a claim that had never been approved, three spoke to the wrong audience, and nobody in the room was sure who could accept the remaining risk.

The prompt was not the bottleneck. The unresolved decisions around the prompt were. Faster drafting had simply delivered more material into the same ambiguity.

More drafts create more decisions

Production capacity once limited the number of options. A campaign team might review two headlines and one body draft. Now it can request dozens of variations across audiences and channels before lunch.

The apparent time saving reappears in the review queue. Someone still has to compare the versions, trace the claims, resolve conflicts with current guidance, and decide which draft deserves attention.

“Can we make another version?” is cheap. “What would make this one publishable?” is the harder question.

The source should be easy to name

A reviewer should be able to trace a product claim, policy statement, price, or proof point without opening six tabs and messaging the person who wrote last quarter’s brief. If the source is obscure, confirmation is slow and later correction is mostly luck.

The first remedy can be ordinary: approved source documents, named owners, a small amount of maintained metadata, and review dates. What matters is having an explicit rule for which source wins when two of them disagree.

Review rules should match the risk

A social variation and a regulated product claim should not wait in the same line. When every draft gets the strictest process, reviewers become a bottleneck. When everything gets the lightest process, the organization is betting that no consequential error will slip through.

A few release tiers are usually enough. Routine material can pass automated checks and a quick editorial look. Pricing, safety, privacy, legal language, and major positioning decisions need the person who owns the consequence. The trigger belongs in the brief, not in a debate after the copy is written.

Quality needs a usable definition

“Make it better” leaves both the system and the reviewer guessing. The criteria need to name what can fail: an unsupported claim, proof that does not match the claim, an audience mismatch, or a call to action the customer cannot actually complete.

Grammar and tone belong on the list, but they should not dominate it. A sentence can be elegant and still be factually weak or strategically wrong.

Accountability stays with people

An evaluator can flag a possible issue. It cannot own the consequence of publication. For the topics that carry real customer or business risk, the decision still needs a name beside it.

That does not mean building a giant approval committee. It means the right person knows when judgment is needed, receives the relevant evidence, and leaves a record of the call.

What I would put in place first

  • A short list of approved sources and an owner for each one.
  • A release tier for each type of content and claim.
  • A quality rubric written in plain language.
  • A record of why a high-risk output was approved or rejected.
  • A feedback route that corrects the source when the same error appears again.

The payoff is not a perfect workflow. It is a review process that spends less time rediscovering the rules and more time on decisions that genuinely require judgment.
