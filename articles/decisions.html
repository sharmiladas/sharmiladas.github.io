The Hard Part of AI Content Is Deciding What Ships

AI can produce a draft in seconds. Teams still have to decide whether the draft is true, useful, safe, and worth publishing.

By Sharmila Das
2026
6 minute read

I have watched teams spend hours improving a prompt when the real disagreement was about the answer itself. Which claim had been approved? Which audience mattered most? How much evidence was enough? Who could accept the risk of publishing?

Those questions existed before generative AI. Faster drafting makes them harder to avoid. A team can now create ten plausible versions before anyone has agreed on the message, the source, or the standard for choosing among them.

More drafts create more decisions

When writing took longer, production capacity limited the number of options. A campaign team might review two headlines and one body draft. The same team can now ask for dozens of variations across audiences and channels.

That sounds efficient until the review begins. Someone must compare the versions, verify the claims, resolve conflicts with current guidance, and decide which one deserves more attention. Generation has moved effort into selection and review.

The useful question is no longer “Can we make another version?” It is “What would make this version ready to publish?”

The source should be easy to name

A reviewer should be able to see where a product claim, policy statement, price, or proof point came from. If the source cannot be named, the team has no dependable way to confirm the answer or update it later.

This does not require every company to build a complex knowledge graph on day one. It can begin with approved source documents, clear owners, useful metadata, and a review date. The important part is that the system knows which material should take priority when sources disagree.

Review rules should match the risk

A low-risk social variation and a regulated product claim do not need the same review path. Teams waste time when every output receives the strictest process. They take unnecessary risk when everything receives the lightest one.

I prefer a small set of release tiers. Low-risk content can pass automated checks and a quick editorial review. Material involving legal, pricing, safety, privacy, or major positioning decisions should reach the person who owns that risk. The trigger should be clear before generation begins.

Quality needs a usable definition

“Make it better” gives an AI system and a reviewer very little help. A useful quality rubric names the decision. Does the draft use an approved claim? Does the proof support the claim? Does the message fit the audience and stage in the journey? Does the call to action match what the customer can actually do?

Grammar and tone still matter. They are only part of the decision. A polished sentence can be strategically wrong or factually weak.

Accountability stays with people

An evaluation model can flag a possible issue. It cannot own the consequence of publishing. Teams need named decision owners for the topics that carry real business or customer risk.

That ownership should feel practical. It means people know when their judgment is needed, what evidence they will receive, and how their decision will be recorded. It does not mean sending every draft to a large approval committee.

What I would put in place first

  • A short list of approved sources and an owner for each one.
  • A release tier for each type of content and claim.
  • A quality rubric written in plain language.
  • A record of why a high-risk output was approved or rejected.
  • A feedback route that corrects the source when the same error appears again.

AI makes production faster. The teams that benefit most will make the surrounding decisions easier to see and easier to repeat.
