How I Would Approach AI Content Quality

A rubric only works when it shapes the knowledge, checks, and decisions behind every asset.

By Sharmila Das
2026
7 minute read

When teams say AI content is good, they often mean it reads smoothly. That is a low bar. A draft can be fluent and still make an unsupported claim, miss the customer’s need, contradict the campaign strategy, or use yesterday’s product information. I would define quality as the ability to publish the right message for the right audience with evidence, clear ownership, and a way to learn from the result.

I would build that standard into the workflow before asking an evaluator to score the final copy. The rubric tells us what good looks like. Rules, source checks, AI evaluators, and human review are different ways to test it.

Start with the brief and the source of truth

A quality test needs something specific to test against. I would turn each request into a structured brief: audience, customer need, journey stage, objective, product, market, channel, intended action, message hierarchy, required proof, approved claims, and risk. Without those fields, a reviewer or model is left judging whether the copy sounds good in isolation.

The generation system would retrieve governed knowledge for that brief: current product facts, audience insights, approved claims and proof, brand guidance, policies, and channel requirements. Each source should have an owner, approval status, effective date, and version. If two sources conflict, the system should expose the conflict rather than let the model quietly choose one.

Use one rubric with twelve distinct questions

I would score content against twelve dimensions, grouped by the decision each one supports:

Strategy. Strategic fidelity and audience relevance: Does this support the objective and speak to the customer’s actual need at this point in the journey?

Evidence. Factual accuracy, grounding and provenance, and freshness: Are claims correct, supported by approved sources, traceable, and current?

Expression. Brand fidelity, message consistency, originality and value, and clarity: Does it preserve the approved message while giving the reader something useful in a clear, appropriate voice?

Delivery. Channel and format fit, safety and policy, and performance readiness: Does it meet the channel’s requirements, respect constraints, and carry the metadata needed to measure outcomes?

The dimensions should be scored separately, with a reason and evidence for each score. A single average can conceal a serious failure. Strategic fidelity, audience relevance, factual accuracy and grounding, and safety or policy would be hard gates. A high overall score cannot compensate for an unsupported claim or the wrong audience.

Match each question to the right check

I would use deterministic checks for requirements that can be measured directly: character limits, required calls to action, approved product names, disclosures, prohibited terms, mandatory metadata, and expired source versions. Claims need extraction and verification against authoritative evidence, with provenance retained alongside the asset.

Judgment-heavy dimensions need a different approach. An AI evaluator can compare a draft with the campaign brief, audience context, message hierarchy, and brand guidance, then explain where it diverges. I would calibrate those evaluations against expert human ratings, monitor disagreements, and retest after model or rubric changes. An evaluator that rewards polished language while missing a strategic mistake is not doing its job.

Human review should follow risk and uncertainty. Routine transformations using approved messages and strong source coverage may pass automated checks with sampling. Novel positioning, pricing or regulated claims, sensitive audiences, unsupported claims, conflicting sources, and low-confidence judgments should go to the appropriate expert. Creative teams retain judgment over expression; the quality system gives them clearer constraints and evidence.

Fix the failed layer, not just the sentence

A failed dimension should produce a targeted repair. If the facts are old, fix source freshness and versioning. If the right information exists but was not retrieved, fix retrieval. If the asset is factually correct but speaks to the wrong customer problem, fix the brief, audience context, or message hierarchy. If the evaluator approves work that experts reject, improve the rubric or its calibration. Regenerating the whole asset for every failure wastes time and hides the real defect.

For each asset I would retain the brief, retrieved sources, evaluator results, rules triggered, human edits, approval decision, and eventual campaign performance. That record lets teams see recurring failures and determine whether the next improvement belongs in knowledge, retrieval, generation, evaluation, or workflow.

Prove that the system improves the work

I would start with a representative set of AI-generated and strategist-created assets and score both against the same rubric. That baseline shows which gaps matter and prevents the team from declaring success merely because drafts are faster. A first pilot could focus on one measurable, manageable-risk use case such as campaign email or web copy.

I would track hard-gate pass rates, unsupported claims, reviewer edits, review time, approval rates, and repeat failure patterns. After publication, I would connect those quality signals with customer and campaign outcomes. If assets score well but perform poorly, the question is whether our rubric captures the strategy and customer value that matter.

My experience informs this approach. In prior content programs, I structured reusable messaging around audience and customer journey; in quality automation work, I translated editorial standards into repeatable checks while leaving judgment with reviewers. The integrated twelve-dimension system described here is how I would bring those lessons together for AI-generated marketing content. Its purpose is not to produce more copy. It is to make trustworthy, strategically useful content repeatable.
