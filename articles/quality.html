AI Quality Starts Before the Final Review

A reviewer can correct one answer. Teams improve reliability when they also correct the conditions that produced it.

By Sharmila Das
2026
6 minute read

The reviewer gets a polished answer, a deadline, and very little else. No source history. No risk label. No explanation of why one piece of evidence was chosen over another. Later, the same review step is blamed for taking too long and for missing errors.

That is too much responsibility at the end of the line. By the time an answer reaches a person, the source, retrieval, context, instructions, and automated checks have already shaped what can be caught—and how much detective work catching it will take.

Separate correction from prevention

Suppose a reviewer catches an outdated product claim and replaces it. The draft is fixed. The system is not. The old claim still needs to be removed or marked, retrieval needs to stop selecting it, and a check needs to catch the same pattern next time.

When that second path is missing, reviewers become human patches for recurring defects. The queue stays busy while the underlying system barely changes.

Define quality for the task

Quality depends on the job. A support answer needs the right steps for the right product version. A marketing brief needs an approved message and evidence that supports it. A policy summary must remain faithful to the rule now in force.

The rubric should capture decisions reviewers already make, in language concrete enough that two people can score the same output and explain a disagreement. If “strategic fidelity” cannot be translated into something observable, it will not guide a review.

A rubric is useful when it tells someone what to inspect, which evidence counts, and what to do after a failure.

Use different checks for different errors

Some checks are mechanical: a required disclaimer appears, a prohibited phrase does not, a link resolves. Others require comparison. A claim can be pulled from the draft and checked against an approved source. Audience relevance may need examples and a scored judgment.

No single evaluator handles all of this equally well. Use the least complicated method that reliably catches the error in question. Adding an LLM judge to a deterministic check usually adds cost and uncertainty, not sophistication.

Keep a small human-rated set

Automated evaluation needs something firmer than its own prior scores. A small set of examples rated by experienced people gives the team a way to see whether the evaluator agrees with the judgments they actually care about.

The most useful examples are awkward ones: near misses, conflicting evidence, and failures people have already seen in production. Refresh the set when the product, policy, or audience changes. Fifty easy examples can create more confidence than insight.

Route review by risk

Human review is expensive when every answer follows the same path and risky when none receives extra scrutiny. Topic, claim type, source conflict, market, and policy sensitivity are more useful routing signals than a blanket “human in the loop” rule.

Routine material can move quickly. Sensitive material should reach the person who understands what an error would cost. Confidence scores can help, but they should not be mistaken for a risk model.

Measure the work around the answer

Accuracy scores tell only part of the story. Review time, substantial edits, repeated failures, and the percentage of corrections that reach the source reveal whether the operation is improving or merely catching mistakes later.

In one quality program, translating editorial standards into explicit author guidance and checks moved measured quality from roughly 65 percent to 85 percent. A representative review dropped from about four hours to one. Reviewers were not working faster; they were spending less time on errors that could have been caught earlier.

A practical review packet

For higher-risk content, the review packet should contain:

  • The generated answer and the original request.
  • The sources used, with dates and owners.
  • The quality results and the reason for any failure.
  • The risk tier and the decision the reviewer is being asked to make.
  • A simple way to send a recurring problem back to the source owner.

The packet does not make judgment automatic. It makes the judgment faster to reach and easier to explain later.
